Ticket Closed. Problem Not Solved.

Your ticket can be closed while the problem is still very much open.

IAN BERKOWITZ · 3 MIN READ

Article

Every MSP report looks the same: tickets opened, tickets closed, response times, a satisfaction score. All green.

Here is what those numbers cannot tell you: whether the same printer mapping has been “fixed” eleven times this year. Whether three different people have reported the same sync problem in three different words, generating three unrelated tickets. Whether your team has quietly stopped reporting the annoying stuff because it never really gets fixed anyway.

A ticket is a unit of activity. A problem is a unit of business friction. They are not the same thing, and a provider paid to close tickets will close tickets.

The test

Pull your last five resolved tickets and ask one question about each: did the cause get fixed, or did the symptom get cleared? A password reset is a symptom. Finding out why lockouts spiked this month is a cause. Remapping a drive is a symptom. Asking why a 200-person company still depends on mapped drives is a cause.

If all five answers are “symptom,” your IT provider is running a queue, not an operation.

What good looks like

Repeat issues get grouped, named, and assigned an owner. Recurring symptoms trigger a root-cause task with a deadline, not another closure. And someone reports to leadership in plain language: here is what kept breaking, here is why, here is what we changed so it stops.

Closed tickets are fine. Closed problems are the job.

— IAN BERKOWITZ, FOUNDER & CEO, IRONGUARD IT

The Three-Minute IT Brief

You do not need more IT tools. You need one operating model.

IAN BERKOWITZ · 3 MIN READ

Article

When something goes wrong repeatedly, the instinct is to buy something: a monitoring tool, a security product, a new portal. Vendors encourage the instinct, because tools are what they sell.

But walk through the actual failures at most 100-to-400-person companies and the pattern is rarely a missing tool. It is a missing owner. Onboarding fails not because HR lacks software but because four teams each own a fragment of it and no one owns the whole path. The security gap is not a missing product—it is a setting nobody has revisited since it was configured, in a tool you already pay for.

What an operating model actually means

It means there is one answer, written down, for each of these questions: How does a new employee get everything they need? What is the standard laptop and when does it get replaced? Buyer’s guide · 10 min read

How to evaluate (or replace) an MSP.

Most companies choose an IT provider three times before they get it right. This guide is the shortcut: what to ask, what the answers mean, and how to switch without breaking anything. Updated September 2026.

The short version: a good MSP can name who owns your outcomes, tells you exactly what is not included in the monthly price, hands over documentation and credentials without a fight, and talks about your business before your firewall. A weak one closes tickets, stacks award badges, and gets vague about scope. The twelve questions below expose the difference in one meeting.

The twelve questions that expose a weak provider

Ask these in the sales meeting, in this order. The wording of the answer matters more than the answer.

  1. “Who, by name, owns our account — and what is their tenure with you?” A rotating queue of anonymous techs is the #1 complaint buyers report after signing.
  2. “What is NOT included in the monthly price?” The single most important question. Vague answers here become surprise invoices later. A serious provider answers in one breath: hardware, travel, projects, and what else.
  3. “Show me your last three out-of-scope invoices to clients like us.” This is where nickel-and-diming hides. Refusal to discuss is your answer.
  4. “Walk me through your last security incident and what you told the client.” Everyone has had one. What you are testing is honesty and communication under pressure.
  5. “Do you outsource or offshore your help desk or engineering?” Not automatically bad — but you should hear it from them, not discover it later.
  6. “Pull five closed tickets and tell me whether the cause was fixed or the symptom cleared.” A provider that measures only ticket closure runs a queue, not an operation.
  7. “Who owns our documentation and credentials, and what happens to them if we leave?” The only acceptable answer: you own everything, handed over completely, no conditions. Provider-held credentials are a hostage situation waiting to happen.
  8. “What do you need from US to do your job?” A provider that asks nothing of you — no standards, no access decisions, no executive time — is planning to be a vendor, not an owner.
  9. “How will you support our sites and field locations, specifically?” Multi-site is where generalist MSPs quietly fail. Ask for their site-launch process.
  10. “What does your quarterly business review contain?” If the answer is ticket counts, that is an activity report, not a business review. You want risks, recommendations, budget, and a roadmap.
  11. “What are your contract length, exit terms, and offboarding process?” Long lock-ins with punitive exits signal a provider that retains clients with contracts instead of performance.
  12. “Which clients like us can we call?” Similar size, similar operational shape. A provider serving 75–500-person multi-site companies should produce these instantly.

Contract red flags

  • “All-inclusive” pricing with an undefined out-of-scope clause — the two cannot coexist.
  • Auto-renewal with a narrow cancellation window buried in the terms.
  • The provider owns the documentation, admin credentials, or licensing agreements for your environment.
  • No written response commitments at all — or commitments with no remedy when missed.
  • Offboarding assistance billed at premium hourly rates, or not defined anywhere.

How to switch without disruption

Fear of a painful transition keeps companies with bad providers for years. A competent incoming provider removes that fear with a phased plan — and runs it while your current provider is still under contract:

  1. Before anything is signed: environment review, access inventory, documentation audit. You learn what the incumbent actually controls.
  2. Weeks 1–2: parallel access established, credentials recovered and rotated, backups verified independently. Nothing user-facing changes yet.
  3. Weeks 3–6: support cutover with both providers overlapping, so no ticket falls in the gap. Employees get one announcement and one new contact route.
  4. Weeks 6–12: stabilization — urgent gaps fixed, environment documented, standards set. Only then does improvement work begin.

The test of the incoming provider is whether they volunteer this plan before you ask. Ironguard’s version of it is the first two phases of how we work — and the working session that starts it is free, because we learn as much from it as you do.

Case study · Managed IT operations

From reactive support to owned IT at a fast-scaling energy company

ANONYMIZED CLIENT · ONGOING ENGAGEMENT

Case study

A rapidly growing, multi-site renewable-energy technology company had outgrown the reactive support model: technology decisions were scattered across vendors, sites, and stretched internal staff while headcount and locations kept climbing. Ironguard took ownership of the full operating model — employee support, Microsoft 365, security baseline, device lifecycle, and vendor management — and turned a collection of point solutions into one accountable system.

The customer

A renewable-energy technology company scaling past 300 people, operating across multiple offices, labs, and field locations in the United States and internationally. Engineering-heavy workforce, Microsoft 365-centered environment, and growth fast enough that every informal process was breaking at once.

The problem

Nothing was on fire — and that was the trap. Tickets got answered. Tools existed. But onboarding went differently every time, device purchasing was ad hoc, licensing had drifted from reality, and every technology question eventually landed on leadership's desk because nobody else owned the whole picture. The company had IT activity without IT ownership.

The business risk

At this scale, the gaps compound: an inconsistent employee lifecycle becomes a security exposure; untracked renewals become budget surprises; each new site launched without a standard becomes a permanent support tax. The real risk was not an outage — it was growth itself turning technology into the company's bottleneck.

Ironguard's assessment

The diagnosis was ownership, not tooling. The environment needed one operating model: a single accountable owner for support, identity, devices, security, and vendors, with standards strong enough that the next hundred hires and the next several sites would land on rails instead of improvisation.

What we did

Ironguard took over day-to-day employee support with clear escalation, then standardized the foundations underneath it: identity and MFA enforcement in Entra ID, device standards with managed enrollment through Intune, a defined onboarding and offboarding path, licensing reconciled to actual usage, and a renewal calendar covering software, hardware, and vendor contracts. Vendor relationships moved behind one throat to choke: ours.

The complexity handled

This was not a greenfield: the work happened while the company kept hiring, opening sites, and running critical operations — across time zones, with an engineering workforce that notices every rough edge. Changes were sequenced so that no team lost a working day to the transition.

The result

Leadership stopped being the escalation path. New hires land with working equipment and correct access on day one. Licensing and renewals stopped generating surprises. And when something breaks, there is one owner, a visible status, and a next step — the operating definition of IT that is actually owned.

Where it stands

The engagement is ongoing: Ironguard continues to own support, security, lifecycle, and planning as the company scales.

Case study · Networks & infrastructure

One design for every site: offices, labs, and field operations on a single standard

ANONYMIZED CLIENT · ONGOING ENGAGEMENT

Case study

Growth had given a multi-site energy organization a portfolio of locations that each worked — differently. Ironguard designed one site standard for networking, connectivity, and support, applied it across offices, labs, and field environments, and made opening the next location a checklist instead of a project crisis.

The customer

The same 300+ person renewable-energy technology company, whose operations span headquarters offices, technical labs, and field locations in the United States and internationally — environments with very different physical constraints and identical business expectations: people show up and everything works.

The problem

Each location had been stood up under deadline by whoever was available, with whatever equipment was on hand. The result was the classic multi-site pattern: different firewall platforms, undocumented closets, connectivity choices nobody could explain, and outage diagnosis that started with twenty minutes of archaeology about how this particular site even worked.

The business risk

For an operations-heavy company, site infrastructure is not back-office — it is the floor the business stands on. Every undocumented site was a single point of failure attached to a single person's memory, and every new location was set to inherit the same debt at the exact moment the company could least afford friction.

Ironguard's assessment

The sites did not need heroics; they needed a standard. One network design — firewall platform, switching, Wi-Fi, segmentation — sized per site type, one connectivity playbook with failover, one documentation rule, and one support model, so that every location becomes a known quantity.

What we did

Ironguard designed the site standard and executed it: audited and documented every existing location, remediated the riskiest gaps first, standardized the network platform, and built the site-launch playbook covering circuits, hardware, cabling, and cutover. Critical infrastructure projects — the work that cannot fail — ran with executive-level ownership from plan through result.

The complexity handled

Field environments punish tidy plans: power, distance, weather, and timelines set by operations rather than IT. The standard was built to survive those constraints — and rollout was sequenced around live operations so that infrastructure work never became the reason a site stood still.

The result

Outage diagnosis starts from documentation instead of archaeology. New locations launch on the standard, on schedule, as a checklist rather than a project crisis. And the question that used to haunt expansion — “what will IT cost us at the new site, and will it work?” — now has a written answer before the lease is signed.

Where it stands

The site standard is now simply how locations get built and supported; Ironguard owns it as part of the ongoing engagement.

Case study · Security & AI governance

A security baseline that survives hypergrowth — identity, devices, and AI governance

ANONYMIZED CLIENT · ONGOING ENGAGEMENT

Case study

Security tooling existed at this growing energy organization — what was missing was ownership of the gaps between the tools, and any governance at all around the AI services employees were rapidly adopting. Ironguard built the security baseline into the daily operating model and gave leadership practical visibility and rules for AI use, without slowing anyone down.

The customer

The same fast-scaling, multi-site renewable-energy technology company: hundreds of employees, a Microsoft 365-centered environment, engineering teams adopting new tools weekly, and customers, partners, and insurers with real security expectations.

The problem

The failure mode was never a missing product. It was MFA with quiet exceptions, devices connecting to company data outside management, offboarding that trailed departures, email protections configured once and never revisited — and, newest of all, AI tools entering the company through individual sign-ups, invisible to leadership, with no rules about what data could go into them.

The business risk

Mid-sized companies rarely get hurt by sophisticated attacks; they get hurt in the unowned space between tools and teams. Add ungoverned AI adoption and the exposure compounds: sensitive data flowing into consumer-grade services with nobody able to answer who is using what.

Ironguard's assessment

Two moves, one discipline. First, make the security baseline part of daily operations — identity, endpoints, email, access lifecycle — each control with a named owner and evidence that it works. Second, treat AI as an operations question: inventory actual usage, set practical rules, and give employees an approved path instead of a prohibition they will route around.

What we did

Enforced MFA and conditional access without exception classes; brought endpoints — including field and remote devices — under management with monitoring; rebuilt email protections to current standards; made offboarding same-day and automatic; and stood up AI governance: a living inventory of tools in use, plain-language rules for what data may go where, an approved-tool path, and periodic review with leadership.

The complexity handled

Security work at a company in motion has one hard constraint: it cannot slow the business that funds it. Controls were rolled out in sequence with communication and exceptions handled to zero — the difference between a baseline on paper and one that holds.

The result

Leadership can now answer the questions that used to end in silence: who has access to what, which devices touch company data, what happens when someone leaves, and which AI tools the company actually uses. Security stopped being a stack of products and became a set of owned, evidenced controls.

Where it stands

The baseline is maintained as part of daily operations, and the AI governance program reviews new tools as the landscape shifts.

Services involved

Identity & access · Endpoint security · Email security · Offboarding automation · AI usage governance · Security reporting
