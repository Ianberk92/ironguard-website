Your new site’s internet should be ordered before the furniture.

Ironguard IT·November 18, 2026·6 min read

The short answer: order the internet circuit the week the lease is signed, schedule cabling before the walls close, and have every piece of network gear configured and labeled a week before anyone moves in. A late opening usually traces back to one of those three dates being missed.

New offices and job sites rarely fail on IT because the work is hard. They fail because IT is scheduled last, and the longest lead-time item on the whole project is the one nobody put on the schedule.

Why IT is the item that slips

The usual order is: sign the lease, design the space, build it out, then call IT. By then the opening date is fixed and the internet provider is quoting weeks.

Carrier lead times are the constraint. Installation timelines for a wired business circuit are often described as about 30 to 60 days, and they run longer when the building needs construction, permits or a landlord’s sign-off to bring service in. None of that can be rushed by anyone on your team. It can only be started earlier.

Everything else in the launch can be compressed with money or overtime. The circuit cannot. So the circuit sets the schedule.

The timeline, working backward from opening day

At lease signing (aim for 90 days out or more)

  • Find out which carriers actually serve the building. Do this before signing if you can. “Fiber in the area” and “fiber in this suite” are different statements.
  • Order the primary circuit. Ask for the install date in writing and what it depends on: construction, permits, landlord access.
  • Pick the site type from your standard. Small office, full office, job-site trailer. Each type should already have a defined kit. If you don’t have a standard yet, this site is where you write it.
  • Name one owner for the IT launch. One person who holds all five dates below.

60 days out

  • Order the network hardware. Firewall, switches, wireless access points, and the licenses they need. Hardware availability moves around, so order when the circuit is ordered, not when the paint is dry.
  • Order a cellular backup connection. It does two jobs: failover for the life of the site, and a way to open on time if the wired circuit slips.
  • Get low-voltage cabling on the contractor’s schedule. Cable drops, access point locations and the network closet need to be decided before the ceilings and walls close.
  • Confirm power and cooling for the closet. A network closet without a dedicated circuit or airflow becomes a recurring outage.

30 days out

  • Configure everything off-site. The firewall, switches and access points should be built to your standard, tested together and labeled before they ever reach the building.
  • Get the people list. Who is moving, who is new, what each person needs on day one: laptop, accounts, access, phone.
  • Handle the things everyone forgets. Printers and plotters, conference room equipment, door access, cameras, and any system the landlord or general contractor controls.
  • Start the site’s documentation. Circuit IDs, carrier support numbers, device names, closet location. Written down before go-live, not after the first outage.

7 days out

  • Install, then test the failure. Unplug the primary circuit and confirm the site stays up on backup. A failover that has never been tested is a hope.
  • Walk the space with a laptop. Check wireless coverage where people will actually sit, including conference rooms and the far corner.
  • Photograph and label the closet. The next person to troubleshoot this site may never have seen it.
  • Tell employees how to get help. Same support path as every other location, from the first morning.

Opening day and the first week

  • Have someone accountable on site or on call. Not a ticket queue. A name.
  • Keep a punch list and close it within the week. The small things left open on day one are the special cases you will be paying for in year two.

Job-site trailers follow the same order, compressed

A trailer or temporary site usually can’t wait for a wired circuit, and often won’t be there long enough to justify one. The approach is the same standard, sized down: cellular or satellite as the primary connection, a small pre-configured kit that ships in one case, the same security settings and the same support path as the main office.

What changes is the lead time, not the discipline. A trailer set up by whoever was on site that day, with whatever router was at the store, becomes the weakest point in the company’s network.

Five dates to put on the project schedule

If you only take one thing from this, add these five lines to the construction or move schedule, each with a named owner:

  1. Circuit ordered. With the carrier’s committed install date next to it.
  2. Cabling rough-in. Before walls and ceilings close.
  3. Hardware on hand. Everything delivered, nothing on backorder.
  4. Staging complete. Configured, tested together and labeled off-site.
  5. Cutover and failover test. At least a week before people arrive.

When those five dates are on the same schedule as flooring and furniture, IT stops being the surprise.

What a missed date costs

You can estimate this for your own site in one line: people at the site × daily cost per person × days they can’t fully work. Add the rent on a space that is open but not usable, and any penalty on the lease you are leaving late.

The larger cost is slower to show up. A site opened in a scramble gets a one-off setup, and one-off setups are the ones that take longest to troubleshoot for as long as the site exists.
